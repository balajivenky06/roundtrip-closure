\section*{Point-by-Point Response --- Reviewer~1}

\rcomment{1}{1}

\begin{reviewerquote}
In Section 5.8, the authors report very low agreement between the LLM judge and human evaluators ($\kappa = 0.074$). This raises concerns about the reliability of the proposed closure evaluation framework. The authors should explain why an LLM judge with such low human agreement is used to determine successful closure and provide additional validation of this approach.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
\textbf{Why used.} \S2.4 now gives three deliberate design reasons: (i)~DeepSeek-R1 is family-disjoint from every pipeline SLM in the DOE, avoiding the self-preference bias documented by \citet{Panickssery2024JudgeBias}; (ii)~the evaluation stack is runnable without proprietary API access (open-weight reproducibility design goal); (iii)~at the coarser within-1 resolution the judge matches the pairwise human agreement rate at $91.7\%$, so for the binary closure gate ($J \geq \rho$) the fine-grained ordinal $\kappa$ is a conservative upper bound on operationally-relevant error.

\textbf{Additional validation (four independent lines).} (i)~Judge-weight sensitivity grid (\S5.6, Table~11): six policies yield mean per-cell closure-rate spread of $0.651$, which is the empirical justification for strict-AND. (ii)~Frontier-judge replay (\S5.8, Table~17): GPT-4o-mini, Claude Haiku~4.5, and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct all attain $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}} \leq 0.199$ against the 5-way human majority --- the slight-band limitation is a general LLM-as-judge property. (iii)~Judge-vs-judge $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ is $0.68$--$0.70$ among frontier judges but only $0.07$--$0.20$ versus humans: LLM judges cluster with each other, not with humans --- a structural signature of a shared bias, not a shared signal. (iv)~The uniform-draw confirmatory study (new, \S5.8) finds matched three-way $\alpha$ rises to $0.439$ (vs.\ stratified $0.376$) and judge-vs-majority $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ rises to $0.157$ (vs.\ $0.138$). The architecture is methodologically aligned with the dual-path LLM-evaluation framework recently proposed by \citet{Han2026CrisisKnowledge} in IP\&M.

\loc{} \S2.4; \S5.6; \S5.8 (frontier replay + uniform-draw); \S8.3.

\rcomment{1}{2}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The human evaluation includes only 60 samples, which is relatively small compared with the 5,890 observations reported in the study. Furthermore, the agreement among human evaluators is low. The authors should consider expanding the human evaluation and explain how these limitations may affect the reliability of the findings.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
Two actions. \textbf{Sample-design clarification:} \S5.8 now explains that the 60-pair sample is a stratified-by-disagreement audit (20 pairs each from frontier/agreement/disputed buckets), not a uniform population sample. Under this design the per-bucket Krippendorff's $\alpha$ 95\% CI half-width is approximately $\pm 0.10$ --- adequate to distinguish ``slight'' from ``moderate'' with high confidence, which is the categorical conclusion the paper draws.

\textbf{Expansion:} we drew a second 60-pair worksheet uniformly at random (seed $20261031$, excluding null cells + the stratified-sample members). The same three author-team annotators re-engaged, blinded to pair identity and judge rating. Both agreement statistics are higher under the uniform draw ($\alpha$: $0.376 \to 0.439$, $+0.063$; judge-vs-majority $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$: $0.138 \to 0.157$, $+0.019$; pairwise inter-annotator $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ in the range $+0.108$ to $+0.196$). The Landis--Koch bands do not change: inter-annotator ``fair'' on both samples, judge-vs-majority ``slight'' on both. The 5-way result ($\alpha = 0.193$, $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}} = 0.074$) that includes external annotators was not replicated under the uniform draw and remains the paper's headline. Full comparison in Table~18 of the revised manuscript.

\loc{} \S5.8 (sample-framing paragraph; new confirmatory uniform-draw study with Table~18).

\rcomment{1}{3}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The study evaluates 20 experimental configurations, including 11 heterogeneous combinations, although many more model combinations are possible. The authors should clearly explain how these configurations were selected and whether their selection could influence the reported results.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
\S4.1 now contains a dedicated DOE-selection paragraph. The 20-cell design is a three-strata purposive sample from the $6^3 = 216$ full stage-model design space:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mono (6 cells, exhaustive):} every open-weight SLM appears as a mono cell M1--M6, so no within-stratum selection bias is possible.
  \item \textbf{Hetero (11 cells, hypothesis-driven):} each cell encodes a specific pre-registered hypothesis (Hypothesis column, Table~2) covering five cross-family pairs, three mixed-strong triples, two weak-plus-strong cells, and one pathology probe (H10). Empirical outcomes --- including H1's per-operator pattern and the H10 phi-4-at-$L_{\textsf{test}}$ pathology --- are findings, not predictions.
  \item \textbf{Null (3 cells):} N1 word-shuffled input; N2 and N3 leave-one-stage-out controls.
\end{itemize}
A formal fractional factorial resolution-III design would require 12--18 cells but lose the per-pair hypothesis coverage; we chose purposive design because the paper's claims are about specific cell behaviours, not marginal main-effects estimation. Where main-effects estimation is required (per-stage mixed-logit, \S5.7), the hetero stratum provides sufficient coverage. All 20~cells and the aligned concept note were committed to source approximately four weeks before the first sweep result.

\loc{} \S4.1 (``DOE selection rationale'' paragraph).

\rcomment{1}{4}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The experiments use 150 functions from HumanEval and MBPP. Although these are established benchmarks, they mainly contain relatively simple Python programming problems. Authors should explain why more complex examples were not considered?
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
Addressed on two levels.

\textbf{Within-benchmark complexity-stratified check.} \S5.11.1 and Table~13 of the revised manuscript split the 150-sample core by function-length quartiles (Q1 $\leq 5$ lines, $n = 46$; Q2 $= 6$--$8$, $n = 36$; Q3 $= 9$--$13$, $n = 33$; Q4 $= 14$--$50$, $n = 35$; median $= 8$, mean $= 9.7$) and report $V_{a,p}$ per quartile for H1 and M3. Three findings: (i)~the H1-vs-M3 advantage on Path~1 is positive at every quartile, with $\Delta_{\text{H1}-\text{M3}}$ ranging $+0.09$ to $+0.18$; (ii)~within-cell $V$ decline from shortest to longest quartile is modest (H1: $-0.104$; M3: $-0.033$); (iii)~Paths~2 and~3 are complexity-stable at the ceiling ($V = 1.000$ at every quartile for both cells). The RQ1 claim is not an artifact of short-function samples.

\textbf{Scope defence.} \S4.2 defends function-level benchmarks on three grounds: (i)~mutation testing (Path~1) is only tractable at function-level granularity; (ii)~the 20-cell sweep was already a six-week single-researcher compute budget --- larger problems would multiply the per-sample token budget by $5$--$10\times$; (iii)~prior cross-stage work \citep{Allamanis2024RTC, Zhang2025CodingTriangle, Li2024MBCT} uses the same benchmarks, so direct comparison requires the same scope. The within-benchmark result does not substitute for a sweep against a larger-problem benchmark; the external-validity caveat in \S8.3 remains in force.

\loc{} \S4.2 (scope paragraph with empirical quartile cuts); \S5.11.1 and Table~13 (new); \S8.3 (external validity).

\rcomment{1}{5}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The manuscript reports that using phi-4 for test generation produces a high percentage of failed tests. This is an interesting finding, but the reasons for these failures are not sufficiently explained. The authors should analyze whether the failures result from incorrect assertions, syntax errors, formatting problems, or other issues.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
\S6.3 adds a forensic breakdown (Table~7, phi-4 failure modes) separating infrastructure errors (HTTP~400 on the phi-4:14b chat endpoint) from genuine test-generation failures:
\begin{itemize}
  \item M2 and H2 show no infrastructure contamination ($0$--$1\%$ HTTP~400). Their $44\%$ and $45\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped} rates are entirely attributable to phi-4 generating syntactically well-formed tests that fail at execution --- i.e.\ assertion failures, not output-format or import errors.
  \item H10 was partially contaminated: $33\%$ of its H10 sweep rows returned HTTP~400 (independent of model capability). After excluding those rows, the clean phi-4-at-$L_{\textsf{test}}$ \textsf{all\_dropped} rate on H10 is $25/50 = 50\%$.
  \item \textbf{Fresh clean H10 re-run (2026-10-30)} under the current Ollama version where the incompatibility is resolved: $38/70 = 54.3\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped}, validating the pathology without infrastructure confound.
\end{itemize}
The mechanism is execution-time test failure, not output artifacts. Per-row AssertionError-vs-TimeoutError categorisation requires the full LLM response cache and is deferred to future work.

\loc{} \S6.3 (forensic breakdown paragraph, Table~7, fresh clean re-run).

\rcomment{1}{6}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The conclusions are based on six SLMs and a limited number of experimental configurations. The authors should clarify whether the identified model-stage relationships are specific to the selected models or likely to apply to other SLM architectures.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We ran a closed-weight ceiling comparison across all three major closed-weight provider families during the revision window, reported in new \S5.12.

Six cells at $n = 60$ per path routed through OpenRouter: M5\_closed (Sonnet~5 mono), H2\_closed and H8\_closed (Sonnet~5 at $L_{\textsf{spec}}$), M7\_gpt (GPT-5.4-mini mono), M9\_gemini (Gemini mono), H9\_gemini (Gemini at $L_{\textsf{spec}}$). Three findings: (i)~\textbf{the pathology replicates across all four provider families}: Microsoft phi-4 ($44$--$54\%$), Anthropic Sonnet~5 ($95\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped} at $L_{\textsf{test}}$), OpenAI GPT-5.4-mini ($67\%$), Google Gemini~2.5~Pro ($57\%$); (ii)~\textbf{family identity is a better predictor than parameter count or benchmark score} --- the three closed-weight models span a $38$-point range despite comparable training compute; (iii)~\textbf{the ceiling premium is modest} ($10$--$15$~pp on favourable paths, not an order of magnitude). The model-stage interaction effect is \emph{not} specific to open-weight SLMs; it generalises to frontier closed-weight models.

\loc{} \S5.12 (closed-weight ceiling comparison with embedded 4-family pathology table); \S7 (updated future-work).

\rcomment{1}{7}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The manuscript uses mutation scores, reference-test results, and semantic similarity to evaluate closure. However, passing a test suite does not necessarily establish complete functional equivalence. The authors should clarify the meaning of closure and avoid interpreting test-based success as proof of full semantic equivalence.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
A dedicated \S3 paragraph (``What `closure' means --- and what it does not'') defines closure as passing the strict-AND two-signal policy at $(\tau = 0, \rho = 3)$: a \emph{probabilistic validity signal} supported by two independent heuristics, \emph{not} a certificate of formal functional equivalence. A worked counter-example (two palindrome functions that pass identical reference tests yet diverge on inputs with multiple palindromic substrings) illustrates the limit. Three explicit implications follow: (i)~our closure signal does not detect divergence on untested inputs; (ii)~strict-AND inherits both components' error rates (quantified in \S5.5 and \S5.8); (iii)~claims about closure \emph{rate differences} across cells remain valid under the probabilistic interpretation. Throughout the paper, ``closure'' is used in this sense; where we need stricter notions we anchor to the human-evaluation protocol (\S5.8).

\loc{} \S3 (new definition paragraph following the strict-AND equations).

\rcomment{1}{8}

\begin{reviewerquote}
Section 6.7 presents design guidelines based on the experimental findings. These guidelines are useful, but their applicability beyond the evaluated models and benchmarks is uncertain. The authors should clarify the conditions under which these recommendations are expected to hold.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
Each guideline in \S6.7 now has an explicit three-condition \emph{Scope conditions} subsection and ``may not hold when'' negative cases: \textbf{G1} (strongest model at bottleneck) holds when one stage is empirically bottleneck, a per-stage capability signal is available, and the pipeline is multi-model; degenerates to ``use the strongest overall model'' otherwise. \textbf{G2} (match test-generator to upstream docstring style) holds for cascaded multi-family pipelines where the downstream stage is style-sensitive (test generation highly so; code synthesis less so, per our data). \textbf{G3} (two-signal validity) holds when an SE-validated metric and an LLM-judge signal are both available at acceptable cost and are not statistically redundant. Each inherits scope limits from the underlying evidence (six open-weight SLMs plus three closed-weight frontier models of \S5.12).

\loc{} \S6.7 (G1, G2, G3 each with Scope-conditions subsection).
