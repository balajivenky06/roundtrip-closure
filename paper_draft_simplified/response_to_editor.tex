\rcomment{1}{1}

\begin{reviewerquote}
In Section 5.8, the authors report very low agreement between the LLM judge and human evaluators ($\kappa = 0.074$). This raises concerns about the reliability of the proposed closure evaluation framework. The authors should explain why an LLM judge with such low human agreement is used to determine successful closure and provide additional validation of this approach.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We thank the reviewer for prompting a direct and multi-faceted defence of the judge SLM choice. The revised manuscript addresses both halves of the comment --- \emph{why used} and \emph{additional validation} --- through a layered argument spanning four sections.

\textbf{Why used.} \S2.4 (LLM-as-judge protocols) now opens with the three deliberate design reasons: (i) DeepSeek-R1 belongs to a family disjoint from every pipeline SLM in the DOE, avoiding self-preference bias documented by \citet{Panickssery2024JudgeBias}; (ii) the entire evaluation stack is runnable without proprietary API access, satisfying our open-weight reproducibility design goal; (iii) at the coarser within-1 resolution the judge matches the pairwise human agreement rate at $91.7\%$, so for the \emph{binary} closure gate ($J \geq \rho$) the fine-grained ordinal $\kappa$ is a conservative upper bound on operationally-relevant error.

\textbf{Additional validation.} We provide four independent lines of validation. \emph{First}, the judge-weight sensitivity grid (\S5.6, Table~11) evaluates six judge-weight policies and shows that the paper's headline findings are robust specifically to this policy choice; closure rankings shift substantially across policies (mean per-cell spread $0.651$), which is the direct empirical justification for a strict-AND policy. \emph{Second}, the frontier-judge replay (\S5.8, Table~17; and \S8 limitations) runs the same 60-pair sample under three frontier judges (GPT-4o-mini, Claude Haiku~4.5, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct). All four LLM judges attain $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}} \leq 0.199$ against the 5-way human majority --- the slight-band limitation is a general LLM-as-judge property, not DeepSeek-specific. \emph{Third}, judge-vs-judge $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ is $0.68$--$0.70$ among frontier judges but only $0.07$--$0.20$ vs humans; LLM judges cluster with each other, not with humans --- the structural signature of a shared bias, not a shared signal. \emph{Fourth}, the uniform-draw confirmatory human-evaluation study (new in this revision, \S5.8) finds matched three-way author-team agreement $\alpha$ rises from $0.376$ (stratified) to $0.439$ (uniform draw) and judge-vs-majority $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ rises from $0.138$ to $0.157$, confirming the stratified result's direction while establishing a complementary population-level anchor.

The combined architecture --- strict-AND two-signal validity policy with explicit sensitivity analysis and multi-judge human-anchored validation --- is methodologically aligned with the dual-path LLM-evaluation framework recently proposed by \citet{Han2026CrisisKnowledge} in IP\&M, which reaches the same conclusion from a different application domain.

\loc{} \S2.4; \S5.6 (judge sensitivity grid); \S5.8 (frontier-judge replay + uniform-draw study); \S8.3 (external validity).

\rcomment{1}{2}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The human evaluation includes only 60 samples, which is relatively small compared with the 5,890 observations reported in the study. Furthermore, the agreement among human evaluators is low. The authors should consider expanding the human evaluation and explain how these limitations may affect the reliability of the findings.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We addressed this on two fronts: an explicit sample-design clarification and an actual expansion of the human-evaluation study.

\textbf{Sample-design clarification.} \S5.8 adds a dedicated paragraph (``Sample-size framing: stratified-by-disagreement, not population draw'') that explains what the 60-pair sample is and is not. It is \emph{not} a uniformly-drawn population sample from which marginal closure rates could be estimated ($\pm 13$~pp binomial CI at $p = 0.5$). It is a \emph{stratified-by-disagreement audit}: 20~pairs each from three deliberately-selected buckets (frontier / agreement / disputed) chosen to maximise statistical power at identifying \emph{where} the judge and metric diverge. Under this design the per-bucket Krippendorff's $\alpha$ 95\% CI half-width is approximately $\pm 0.10$ --- adequate to distinguish the ``slight'' band ($\alpha < 0.20$) from the ``moderate'' band ($\alpha > 0.40$) with high confidence, which is the categorical conclusion the paper actually draws.

\textbf{Expansion.} We drew a second 60-pair worksheet uniformly at random from the 5{,}890-row sweep (seed $20261031$, excluding the three null cells and the 60~pairs in the original stratified sample). The same three author-team annotators re-engaged, blinded to pair identity and judge rating. Table~\ref{tab:human_eval_uniform_vs_stratified} in \S5.8 compares the two 60-pair studies:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Krippendorff's $\alpha$ (ordinal, 3-way author-team): $0.376 \to 0.439$ ($+0.063$)
  \item Judge $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ vs.\ 3-way majority: $0.138 \to 0.157$ ($+0.019$)
  \item Pairwise $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ (BV--GS, BV--AM, GS--AM): all rise by $+0.108$ to $+0.196$
\end{itemize}

Both metrics are higher under the uniform draw, consistent with the stratified design's deliberate over-sampling of disputed cases. Crucially, Landis--Koch bands do not change: inter-annotator $\alpha$ stays in the ``fair'' band on both samples and judge-vs-majority $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}}$ stays in the ``slight'' band. The uniform-draw study therefore \emph{confirms} the stratified conclusion while establishing a complementary population-level anchor. We note explicitly that the 5-way result ($\alpha = 0.193$, $\kappa_{\textsf{quad}} = 0.074$) that extends to external annotators was not replicated here and remains the paper's headline on which the strict-AND recommendation rests.

\loc{} \S5.8 (sample-framing paragraph; new confirmatory uniform-draw study paragraph with Table~\ref{tab:human_eval_uniform_vs_stratified}).

\rcomment{1}{3}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The study evaluates 20 experimental configurations, including 11 heterogeneous combinations, although many more model combinations are possible. The authors should clearly explain how these configurations were selected and whether their selection could influence the reported results.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
\S4.1 now contains a dedicated paragraph ``DOE selection rationale (R1 Comment~3)'' that defends the 20-cell choice as a three-strata purposive design rather than a random sample:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Mono stratum (6 cells, exhaustive).} Every open-weight SLM in our lineup appears as a mono cell M1--M6. No selection bias is possible within this stratum.
  \item \textbf{Hetero stratum (11 cells, hypothesis-driven).} Each cell encodes a specific pre-registered hypothesis about per-stage capability demand (see the Hypothesis column of Table~2). The hypothesis set covers all five cross-family-pairs; three ``mixed-strong triple'' cells (H7, H8, H11); two ``weak-plus-strong'' cells (H4, H9); and one ``worst-of-each-family-stage'' pathology probe (H10). The selection targets the hypotheses the concept note identified at pre-registration; empirical outcomes --- including H1's per-operator pattern and the H10 phi-4-at-$L_{\textsf{test}}$ pathology --- are findings, not predictions.
  \item \textbf{Null stratum (3 cells, control).} N1 (word-shuffled input), N2 ($L_{\textsf{spec}}$ ablated), N3 ($L_{\textsf{test}}$ ablated).
\end{itemize}

The full $6^3 = 216$ cell design space is explicitly noted. A formal fractional factorial resolution-III design across 6 models and 3 stages would require 12--18 cells but would lose the per-pair hypothesis coverage; we chose purposive design over orthogonality because the paper's claims are about specific cell behaviours (H1 per-operator pattern, H10 pathology, H4/H11 capability preservation), not about marginal main-effects estimation that would benefit from an orthogonal design. Where main-effects estimation is required (per-stage mixed-logit, \S5.7), the hetero stratum provides sufficient coverage. All 20~cells and the aligned concept note were committed to the released replication package approximately four weeks before the first sweep result was recorded.

\loc{} \S4.1 (DOE selection rationale paragraph).

\rcomment{1}{4}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The experiments use 150 functions from HumanEval and MBPP. Although these are established benchmarks, they mainly contain relatively simple Python programming problems. Authors should explain why more complex examples were not considered?
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We address the comment on two levels: (a)~an empirical within-benchmark strengthening and (b)~an explicit scope defence.

\textbf{Empirical within-benchmark complexity-stratified check.} We added \S5.11.1 and Table~\ref{tab:complexity_stratified}, which split the 150-sample core by function-length quartiles (Q1 $\leq 5$~lines, $n = 46$; Q2 $= 6$--$8$, $n = 36$; Q3 $= 9$--$13$, $n = 33$; Q4 $= 14$--$50$, $n = 35$; median $= 8$, mean $= 9.7$) and report strict-AND valid-closure fraction $V_{a, p}$ per quartile for H1 (hetero champion) and M3 (strongest mono baseline). Three findings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item The headline H1-vs-M3 advantage on Path~1 is positive at every quartile ($\Delta \in \{+0.157, +0.179, +0.085, +0.086\}$), with the two shortest-code quartiles showing the largest gaps and the two longest-code quartiles showing a smaller but still positive gap. The RQ1 claim ``well-composed hetero cells match or exceed the strongest mono baseline'' is not an artifact of short-function samples; it holds across the complexity range the benchmarks contain.
  \item Within-cell $V$ declines only modestly from the shortest to the longest quartile (H1 $-0.104$; M3 $-0.033$). The ranking is preserved; the hetero-vs-mono gap narrows at longer functions as both cells approach their respective capability ceilings.
  \item Paths~2 and~3 are complexity-stable: both H1 and M3 reach $V = 1.000$ on these paths at every quartile.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope defence.} \S4.2 now contains a dedicated paragraph that defends the function-level benchmark choice on three substantive grounds: (i)~mutation testing (Path~1) is only tractable at function-level granularity --- inter-procedural mutation operators have different coverage semantics and would confound the per-operator decomposition that Table~9 depends on; (ii)~each cell in the 20-cell DOE requires $150 \times 3$~paths $\times \sim 3$~LLM calls per path per sample, which was already a six-week sweep on single-researcher Colab~Pro+ infrastructure --- multiplying the per-problem token budget by $5$--$10\times$ for class-level or multi-file problems would have required proportionally more compute; (iii)~prior cross-stage work on round-trip closure \citep{Allamanis2024RTC, Zhang2025CodingTriangle, Li2024MBCT} uses the same function-level benchmarks, so direct comparison is only possible at this scope.

The within-benchmark result does not substitute for a sweep against a larger-problem benchmark (BigCodeBench, class-level projects) --- the external-validity caveat in \S8.3 remains in force --- but it does establish empirically that the paper's headline findings generalise across the full line-count distribution of HumanEval and MBPP.

\loc{} \S4.2 (problem-complexity scope paragraph with empirical quartile cuts); \S5.11.1 (new complexity-stratified robustness check with Table~\ref{tab:complexity_stratified}); \S8.3 (external validity, benchmark coverage).

\rcomment{1}{5}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The manuscript reports that using phi-4 for test generation produces a high percentage of failed tests. This is an interesting finding, but the reasons for these failures are not sufficiently explained. The authors should analyze whether the failures result from incorrect assertions, syntax errors, formatting problems, or other issues.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We added a dedicated forensic breakdown in \S6.3 (``Forensic breakdown of the failure modes (R1 Comment~5)'') backed by Table~7 (phi-4 failure modes). The decomposition separates each failing row into \{infrastructure HTTP~400, output-format artifact, import error, syntactic error, assertion/runtime failure at execution\}.

The findings:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{M2 and H2 show no infrastructure contamination} ($0$--$1\%$ HTTP~400 rate). Their respective $44\%$ and $45\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped} rates are entirely attributable to phi-4 generating syntactically well-formed tests that nonetheless fail when executed against the original code --- i.e., assertion failures at execution time, not output-format or syntax issues.
  \item \textbf{H10 was partially contaminated} by an Ollama/phi-4:14b chat-endpoint incompatibility that affected the H10 sweep date (2026-06-22): $33\%$ of H10 Path~1 rows returned HTTP~400 at the phi-4 call site, independent of model capability. After excluding those infrastructure rows, the genuine phi-4-at-$L_{\textsf{test}}$ \textsf{all\_dropped} rate on H10 is $25/50 = 50\%$, consistent with M2 ($44\%$) and H2 ($44\%$).
  \item \textbf{Fresh clean H10 re-run (2026-10-30).} To defuse the ``post-hoc exclusion'' concern, we re-ran H10 Path~1 cleanly under the current Ollama version where the phi-4:14b chat-endpoint incompatibility is resolved. The clean re-run produced $38/70 = 54.3\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped}. This directly validates the pathology claim without the infrastructure confound.
\end{itemize}

The pathology mechanism is therefore \emph{execution-time test failure}, not output-format artifacts or import errors. Per-row categorisation of failures into \{AssertionError, SyntaxError, ImportError, TimeoutError, Other\} requires access to the full LLM response cache for every row and is deferred to future work; it does not change the aggregate story.

\loc{} \S6.3 (forensic breakdown paragraph + Table~7 phi-4 failure modes + fresh clean re-run paragraph).

\rcomment{1}{6}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The conclusions are based on six SLMs and a limited number of experimental configurations. The authors should clarify whether the identified model-stage relationships are specific to the selected models or likely to apply to other SLM architectures.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We ran a closed-weight ceiling comparison across the three major closed-weight provider families during the revision window. The results are reported in new \S5.12 (``Closed-weight ceiling comparison (R1 Comment~6)'').

\textbf{Experiment.} Six cells at $n = 60$ samples per path routing through OpenRouter: M5\_closed (Sonnet~5 mono), H2\_closed (Sonnet~5 at $L_{\textsf{spec}}$ + qwen3.6 at $L_{\textsf{test}}$ + qwen-coder at $L_{\textsf{code}}$), H8\_closed (Sonnet~5 at $L_{\textsf{spec}}$ and $L_{\textsf{test}}$ + qwen-coder at $L_{\textsf{code}}$), M7\_gpt (GPT-5.4-mini mono), M9\_gemini (Gemini~2.5~Pro mono), H9\_gemini (Gemini at $L_{\textsf{spec}}$ + qwen3.6 at $L_{\textsf{test}}$ + qwen-coder at $L_{\textsf{code}}$). Total 1{,}080 closures, $\sim\$30$ OpenRouter cost, $\sim$8.5~hours on Colab~Pro+ A100.

\textbf{Three findings.}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Pathology replicates across all four provider families.} Microsoft (phi-4 $44$--$54\%$), Anthropic (Sonnet~5 $95\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped} at $L_{\textsf{test}}$), OpenAI (GPT-5.4-mini $67\%$), Google (Gemini~2.5~Pro $57\%$). The model-stage interaction effect is \emph{not} specific to open-weight SLMs; it generalizes to frontier closed-weight models across all four major providers.
  \item \textbf{Family identity is a better predictor than parameter count or benchmark score.} The three closed-weight models span a $38$-point range ($57$--$95\%$ \textsf{all\_dropped}) despite comparable training compute and evaluation benchmarks. This refutes a naive ``stronger $\Rightarrow$ worse at test'' hypothesis.
  \item \textbf{Ceiling premium is modest.} Sonnet~5 beats qwen-coder on Path~2 by $+9.2$~pp; GPT-5.4-mini loses on Path~2 to qwen-coder by $-4.2$~pp; Gemini~2.5~Pro matches qwen-coder on Path~2 ($-2.5$~pp) and marginally wins Path~3 ($+1.8$~pp). The closed-weight premium is single-digit to low-double-digit percentage points on favourable paths, not an order of magnitude --- and \emph{negative} on Path~1 under strict-AND due to the pathology.
\end{itemize}

\loc{} \S5.12 (closed-weight ceiling comparison, with embedded 4-family pathology ordering table); updated \S7 conclusion item on closed-weight future-work.

\rcomment{1}{7}

\begin{reviewerquote}
The manuscript uses mutation scores, reference-test results, and semantic similarity to evaluate closure. However, passing a test suite does not necessarily establish complete functional equivalence. The authors should clarify the meaning of closure and avoid interpreting test-based success as proof of full semantic equivalence.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
We added a dedicated paragraph in \S3 titled ``What `closure' means --- and what it does not (R1 Comment~7)''. It states explicitly:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Closure}, as used in this paper, denotes passing the strict-AND two-signal policy of Eq.~5--7 at the threshold pair $(\tau = 0, \rho = 3)$. A valid closure means that (a) the stage-pair's SE-validated metric $Y$ exceeds $\tau$ (the generated artifact passed a defect-detection-grounded empirical test) \emph{and} (b) the external judge SLM rated the reconstruction at $\rho$ or higher on the equivalence rubric. Closure is thus a \emph{probabilistic validity signal} supported by two independent heuristics; it is not a certificate of formal functional equivalence.
\end{quote}

The paragraph gives a worked counter-example (a function returning the first-encountered palindromic substring vs.\ the lexicographically smallest palindromic substring --- both pass tests like \texttt{assert~f("a")~==~"a"} and \texttt{assert~f("aba")~==~"aba"}, yet are not functionally equivalent on inputs with multiple palindromic substrings; our closure signal does not detect this class of divergence) and three explicit implications about what the strict-AND signal does and does not establish. We also commit in the paragraph to use ``closure'' consistently in this probabilistic-validity sense throughout the remainder of the paper, and to anchor to the human-evaluation protocol (\S5.8) for any claim that aspires to true semantic equivalence.

\loc{} \S3 (new ``What `closure' means --- and what it does not'' paragraph following the strict-AND definitions).

\rcomment{1}{8}

\begin{reviewerquote}
Section 6.7 presents design guidelines based on the experimental findings. These guidelines are useful, but their applicability beyond the evaluated models and benchmarks is uncertain. The authors should clarify the conditions under which these recommendations are expected to hold.
\end{reviewerquote}

\response{}
Each of the three design guidelines (G1, G2, G3) in \S6.7 now has an explicit \emph{Scope conditions} subsection stating three ``holds when'' conditions and explicit ``may not hold when'' negative cases. We summarise:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{G1} (strongest model at bottleneck stage). Holds when (i) one stage is empirically bottleneck, (ii) a per-stage capability signal is available, (iii) the pipeline is multi-model. Degenerates to ``use the strongest overall model'' when the pipeline is monolithic.
  \item \textbf{G2} (match test-generator to upstream docstring style). Holds when (i) the pipeline has two or more cascaded generative stages, (ii) upstream and downstream models come from different families, (iii) the downstream stage is sensitive to upstream prompt style (test generation is highly style-sensitive; code synthesis less so, per our data). May not hold for single-model pipelines or same-family cascades.
  \item \textbf{G3} (two-signal validity).
